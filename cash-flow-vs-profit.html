Cash Flow vs Profit: Why Profitable Businesses Still Run Out of Money

  • Finance Advisory
  • 08.OCT.2026
  • 4 MIN READ

It surprises many business owners: the accounts show a profit, sales are growing, and yet there is not enough money in the bank to pay suppliers, rent or salaries this month. How can a profitable business run short of cash?

The answer lies in the difference between profit and cash flow. Understanding it is one of the most useful things any business owner can learn.

What is the difference?

Profit is what is left when you subtract your expenses from your sales over a period of time. It shows whether your business model works and makes money over time.

Cash flow is the actual money moving in and out of your bank account, and when it moves. It shows whether you can pay your bills on time.

Profit tells you if your business is healthy in the long run. Cash flow tells you if it can survive this month.

A sale counts towards profit when you make it, even if the customer pays you 60 days later. But you cannot pay this week's salaries with money you will receive in two months.

Why profitable businesses run out of cash

These are some of the most common reasons the bank balance and the profit figure tell different stories.

1. Customers pay late

If you give customers 30, 60 or 90 days to pay, or they simply pay late, your profit appears in the accounts long before the cash arrives. Meanwhile, you still have to pay your own costs.

2. Fast growth

Growth sounds like good news, but it usually needs cash first. You may need more stock, more staff and more equipment before the extra sales are paid for. Many businesses run into trouble at exactly the moment they are growing fastest.

3. Too much money tied up in stock

Products sitting in a warehouse are money you have already spent but cannot use. Buying in large quantities to get a discount can look smart on paper but leave you short of cash.

4. Large one-off payments

Equipment purchases, loan repayments, annual fees and taxes can take a large amount of cash out of the business at once. If these are not planned for, they can cause a sudden shortage.

5. Owner withdrawals

Taking money out of the business based on profit, without checking how much cash is actually available, can quickly leave the business without enough to operate.

How to keep your cash flow healthy

  • Make a simple cash-flow forecast. List the money you expect to come in and go out each week or month for the next three to six months. This shows shortfalls before they happen.
  • Invoice quickly and follow up. Send invoices as soon as work is done, make payment terms clear and follow up politely but firmly on late payments.
  • Agree on payment terms with suppliers. Where possible, align when you pay suppliers with when your customers pay you.
  • Watch your stock levels. Keep enough to serve customers, but avoid tying up cash in slow-moving items.
  • Build a cash buffer. Aim to keep a reserve to cover a few months of essential costs for unexpected situations.
  • Plan for large payments. Put taxes, loan repayments and big purchases in your forecast so they never come as a surprise.

The bottom line

Profit and cash flow are both important, but they are not the same thing. Keeping an eye on both, with regular reports and a simple forecast, helps you avoid unpleasant surprises and make better decisions about growth, hiring and investment.

If your finances feel unclear or you often worry about paying the bills, a financial adviser can help you set up simple reporting and forecasting so you always know where your business stands.
